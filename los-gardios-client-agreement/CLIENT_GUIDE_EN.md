## Chapter 1 — How Los Gardios Works

### The Guiding Principle: The Client Sets the Framework

The Organization's core operating principle is simple: the Client defines the financial and strategic framework in which it wants to, and can, operate, and the Organization examines what can responsibly be built within it — and only then, together, do we arrive at a concrete plan and commercial model. This is the opposite of the common model, in which a vendor decides in advance how much the client must pay. Chapter 3 (Budget, Investment Framework and Timeline) explains this principle in depth, including how it plays out in practice.

### Who Decides What — the Client and the Organization

The principle above gives rise to a clear division of roles, which recurs and plays out throughout the engagement — from the financial framework (Chapter 3) to day-to-day execution (Chapter 9):

| What you decide | What the Organization examines and decides |
|---|---|
| The project's vision and business goal (Chapter 3) | Whether the goal is feasible within the framework you defined (Chapter 3) |
| Your sustainable investment capacity — monthly budget, total scope and range of flexibility (Chapter 3) | The operating structure and methodology through which the framework you set is implemented — the outputs of Genesis Research (Chapter 4) |
| The timeframe you plan to operate in (Chapter 3) | The allocation of resources and the recommended deployment within the budget and scope boundaries you set |
| Your Tolerance level and Tolerance period (Chapter 8), and financial boundaries, if any (Chapter 3) | The sequence of execution and the day-to-day operating decisions, within those same boundaries (Chapter 9) |

**Good to know:** this division does not limit your right to oversee, ask questions and request reporting at any time (Chapter 9) — it only clarifies where the Organization's professional judgment begins, once you have defined the framework within which it operates.

### Foundations of the Cost of Protecting the Organization's Assets

**Illustrative example — why the floor is the same even for a small budget.** A client with a monthly budget of only ₪15,000: if the Organization representative who was solicited to work with that client directly leaves after two months, the cost of sourcing, screening and training that the Organization has already paid is gone — the same whether the engagement was worth ₪15,000 or ₪150,000 a month. That is why the floor (₪150,000 here) does not go down with the budget: it protects a cost that has already been paid, rather than "penalizing" a small budget. It is the multiplier (6× the Average Monthly Consideration) that grows with the size of the engagement — not the floor.

## Chapter 2 — Engagement Models

The final financial consideration for each Specific Engagement is set and signed in a separate "Proposal", once Genesis Research is complete (see Chapter 4) — but it is worth getting to know, already now, the commercial-model frameworks the Agreement supports.

### The Four Tracks

**Track 0 — Research and strategy purchase, with no ongoing budget.** Suited to a client that does not allocate an ongoing budget and scope boundaries. Genesis Research and the plan it produces can be purchased for a one-time payment, at a base price of US$10,000 plus VAT (Appendix A, Section 2) — the same research, and the same value, as in the budget-based tracks; the only difference is how it is financed. **Track B — Hybrid model.** A combination of a reduced retainer and value-sharing (a results-/percentage-based component). Suited when there is a wish to share risk and opportunity between the Parties, without giving up a baseline of payment certainty entirely.

**Track C — Results-based model.** The Organization's consideration is based mainly on value-sharing/percentages, subject to a mechanism to be set in the Proposal. Suited when both Parties want the Organization's financial participation to be tied directly to the outcome actually created.

In any percentage-based or hybrid track: the details of the exact mechanism — what the percentage applies to, what triggers it, what the settlement period is, and whether there is a floor/cap — will be set and recorded in the signed "Proposal" itself; Appendix B to the Agreement sets only the general principles (for example, that there is a budget, scope boundaries, Tolerance and a Stop-Loss), not the specific parameters or the final structure of the consideration.

**Not sure which track fits you?** You can mark "to be set in the Proposal" and ask for the Organization's recommendation (see "Two Possible Paths" in Chapter 3) — Genesis Research is carried out in the same way and at the same fixed price, which does not depend on the track, and the final track is set in this record when the Proposal is signed.

### If You End Early, on Your Own Initiative — the Exit Schedule

In a budget-based engagement (Tracks A/B/C), the Organization invests in building the plan and the activity before that investment is recovered through the ongoing consideration — just as in most long-term supplier–client relationships. If you initiate the termination of the engagement (not the Organization, and not as a result of a breach on its part) before the originally defined period has ended, your signed Proposal may include an **Exit Schedule** — a declining, tiered amount that reflects the investment not yet recovered by the Organization, the earlier the engagement ends. The longer the engagement continues, the smaller the amount, until it reaches zero. The Exit Schedule, if there is one, is set out in figures in the Proposal itself — it is not predicted here in advance; and if your Proposal does not include an Exit Schedule, no such charge will apply to you at all (Section 13(e) of the Agreement). Nor does it apply if it was the Organization that initiated the termination, if the engagement ended because of a breach on its part, or in circumstances of force majeure.

## Chapter 3 — Budget, Investment Framework and Timeline

### Why We Ask About This

We don't start from "here is our package and its price". We start from: tell us what you are trying to achieve, how much you are truly willing and able to invest, what constraints exist, and what you believe is possible. We will then determine what can responsibly be built within that framework.

Before we build a business, marketing and financial plan for you, we need to understand the financial reality in which you want, and are able, to operate. This information is not meant to determine how much we can charge you — it is meant to determine what can responsibly be built, at the scope, pace and model that suit your business.

A larger budget may allow a wider scope, more resources, additional channels or a faster pace — but not a better outcome in and of itself; the link between budget and scope of work is a matter of planning, not a promise. **And the level of attention, professionalism and investment we bring to your project does not depend on the size of the budget** — it is the same at every scale of work.

### Our Model: You Set the Framework

Our guiding rule is simple, and plays out in three stages: **you** define the financial framework in which you want to operate — budget, goals, constraints. **We** examine what can responsibly be built within that framework. Only then, **together**, do we arrive at a concrete plan and commercial model — not before. This is the deliberate opposite of the industry's common approach, in which a vendor tells a client how much to pay before it even understands the client's business.

Sometimes, after examining the framework you have defined, we will conclude that it does not match the scope of the project you are asking for. In that case we will hold a good-faith conversation with you before any decision on how to proceed — we will not build you a Proposal we do not believe is sustainable. Our goal is an engagement you can sustain over time — not one that pushes you beyond your means.

### Our Trust Starts with Candor

**Give us the real number — not the number you think we want to hear.** Most clients worry about two opposite mistakes: giving a number that is too small and looking unserious, or giving a number that is too large to justify the vision. Both mistakes lead to the same result — a plan that does not truly fit you. There is a practical consequence, too: after signing, the budget you set becomes an operating commitment for at least three months (see "Your Investment Framework" below) — so an inflated number does not make your project more impressive; it becomes a real commitment you will have to meet. There is no "too small" number here — only an inaccurate one. The quality of the strategy depends on the quality of the information available to us to build it, and we ask you to be open with us, just as we are open with you about what we believe is realistic.

**A big vision, a small budget for now — that is exactly the situation we have a structure for.** Budget is a question of where the business is today; opportunity is a question of where it can go. These are two separate axes, not one. A business with exceptional potential, limited liquidity for now, a unique market opportunity or a strong founder's vision may justify a tailored commercial structure just as much as a business with a large budget may not suit us at all — we look at potential, reality, resources, commitment and strategic fit, not only at the size of the budget. Where that gap exists, we have commercial structures built precisely for it (see "Growth Modes", Chapter 8) — an initial, reduced scope that grows gradually, a percentage-based structure, or phasing. These are not goodwill discounts — they are commercial architecture. But to be able to use them, we need your real current budget — not a number that tries to "match" the size of the vision.

## Chapter 7 — The Break-Even Point

### The Principle That Guides Us: Not Spending It All

Let us say it openly: we have a clear interest in not pushing you to spend your entire monthly budget. A client who has exhausted its cash flow does not keep investing next quarter — in other words, spending everything today harms the client in the long run for the sake of a bigger invoice in the short run, and that serves neither of us. This is not only a values position on our part; it is also the economic logic that drives us to preserve the business's ability to keep investing, operating and growing.

And that is also why our operating default, in every engagement, is to operate **at break-even** rather than at full budget spend — not just as a sentence here, but written into the mechanism itself (see "the Break-Even point" below). Not every short term is worth spending everything.

### The Break-Even Point — What It Is

**What this means for you in practice:** think of the Break-Even point as a fuel gauge, not a profit target. As long as the cost of acquiring a customer does not exceed what that customer is worth to you on average — we are in the safe range. That does not mean there is a large net profit; it means we are not burning money to bring in customers. It is a planning tool, **not a promise of profitability and not a guaranteed forecast** — so it does not "lock" you into an outcome; it only helps both of us know when we are in the healthy range.

In precise terms: the Break-Even point is a planning reference point — as long as the actual acquisition cost does not exceed the average transaction value multiplied by your gross margin, the activity is considered to be at or above break-even. The full, binding definition — Appendix B, Section 2(e) of the Agreement; a different, explicit definition can also be set in the signed Proposal, and will prevail.

The two figures that feed the formula are different in nature, including in what happens if you leave them blank:

| Field | Default if not provided |
|---|---|
| Gross margin (before fixed expenses) | **No default.** A figure only you know; if it is not provided, the Break-Even mechanism as a whole will not be activated (only the general limit on budget overruns will remain in effect) |
| Average order value (AOV) | **There is a default** — the value to be determined in your Genesis Research |

Your gross margin and average order value (AOV) are collected in the Intake Form; if the gross margin is not provided, the Break-Even mechanism as a whole will not be activated, and if the AOV is not provided, we will rely on Genesis Research.

Also good to know: the Break-Even point is a floor, not a ceiling — you can, and should, also set additional profitability targets above it, if you have any (you can note this in the Intake Form, as well as an alternative Break-Even definition instead of the default, if you prefer). To the extent the Break-Even point and the relevant financial definitions are recorded in the Agreement and in the signed Proposal — they are what will bind, not the general explanations here.

## Chapter 8 — Growth Modes

This chapter brings together the mechanisms through which the engagement can express a shared intention to grow beyond the Break-Even point (Chapter 7): how much "breathing room" you are willing to give the model, which engagement structures support gradual growth, and how a value-dependent consideration structure (Track B/C) may align the Organization's economic interest with yours. These three mechanisms are independent of one another, and each is relevant to a different degree depending on the commercial structure you choose.

### Your Tolerance Level and Period

**In simple terms:** you have a period, agreed in advance, in which we are allowed to build and test without chasing immediate profit — but it comes with two built-in brakes that protect you. The first brake: even during this period, the default is that we operate at break-even — that is, we do not cause you a loss on purpose, even if there is no profit yet. The second brake: if you expressly choose to allow us to go below break-even to accelerate building, a cap agreed in advance (Stop-Loss) will always be set, which may not be exceeded. In other words: you decide how much "breathing room" to give the model, and we do not allow an overrun beyond the cap you set to continue — crossing the threshold stops or scales back the activity within one Business Day (see Chapter 9, "What Happens if the Stop-Loss Threshold Is Crossed"), taking into account routine, non-material daily volatility.

For example: a budget of ₪20,000 a month and a Tolerance period of five months mean that during that period, we may use the available budget to build, test and expand the activity — even if it is not yet profitable, and always within the brakes above.

**The default: break-even, not loss.** Even within the Tolerance period, the ongoing target is to operate at the Break-Even point (see the explanation in Chapter 7) — not to generate artificial profit, and not to generate a loss on purpose. Tolerance gives flexibility around the profitability expectation; it is not permission to lose money.

**A temporary deficit — only if you explicitly choose it, and always with a cap.** Some clients prefer to direct the entire budget toward building and expansion in the first period, even if that means a temporary operating deficit and not merely an absence of profit. This is a legitimate business preference — but it differs from the default, and will be stated explicitly if it is what you want, with a framework and a cap agreed in advance. The cap that is set is the operating Stop-Loss threshold for that period (Appendix B, Section 3(a)) — not an additional mechanism on top of it — so the maximum permitted overrun is clear and agreed in advance in every case.

Your preferred Tolerance period, the Tolerance level (Conservative / Balanced / Aggressive / Maximum Expansion — Scale), your willingness to run a temporary operating deficit (and if so, the requested cap), and the measurement period of the Stop-Loss threshold (if you do not want the default of a loss equal to one month's budget, over 30 days — Appendix B, Section 3(a)) — all of these are collected in the Intake Form.

Good to know: this does not mean you are giving up any right. At any time, even within the Tolerance period, you can stop activity, review results and end the engagement — subject to the advance-notice period set in the Proposal (default: 30 days, Section 13(b) of the Agreement) and to the Exit Schedule, if one was set (see "the Exit Schedule", Chapter 2) — nothing here suspends, limits or defers those rights. And as always — even at the end of the period, this is no promise of an outcome or of profitability (see also the clarification on the Break-Even point in Chapter 7). The Tolerance level and period, as well as an agreed temporary deficit if one was agreed, will be recorded in the signed Engagement Proposal; none of them creates a new legal mechanism beyond what is set out in the Agreement.

### Commercial Flexibility — Architecture, Not a Discount

When the opportunity, the strategic fit and the expected long-term value justify it, the Organization may structure its participation in the engagement differently — for example, an initial, reduced scope that grows gradually, or a phased engagement. These are engagement structures that the legal documents support, not discounts and not a gesture of goodwill — this is commercial architecture, designed to fit the engagement to the reality of the business.

### Shared Growth — to the Extent the Commercial Structure Allows

When the commercial structure includes a value-dependent component (Track B or C), the Organization's economic interest is partly aligned with yours — the more successful the project, the greater the Organization's economic participation in it. That is why the Organization may prefer such a structure when it has high confidence in the project's potential. That said, this describes a structural incentive — not a promise of an outcome or of shared success; actual performance depends on many factors, as set out later in this Guide.

## Chapter 9 — Execution and Ongoing Reporting

### How the Engagement Works

The engagement between the Client and the Organization is a commercial transaction between two business entities (B2B) — not an employment, agency, partnership or joint-venture relationship; each Party is fully responsible for its own employment terms and taxation. The Organization performs the services through its representatives, employees, partners and subcontractors, and bears full responsibility for their performance.

Each Party acts through a Primary Authorized Representative and an Alternate Authorized Representative; an instruction or approval from either of them binds the Party that appointed them. So that both Parties can rely on what was agreed, approval of a deliverable, a professional instruction or a change to the terms of the engagement is given, and binds, only in a form that can be shown in writing — an email, an entry in the Neuron system (see Chapter 6), a recorded call or meeting, or a meeting summarized in writing and approved by the representatives of both Parties.

### Ongoing Oversight and Reporting

So that you can follow the project without being involved in every day-to-day operating decision, the Agreement sets out several standing oversight mechanisms — worth getting to know already at this stage:

- **Ongoing reporting.** You will receive from us at least one concise monthly performance report and at least one quarterly strategic review, showing actual spend, results and profitability/Break-Even metrics against the plan (Appendix B, Section 2(c)). Reports are delivered in writing, through the Neuron system (see Chapter 6, "Proprietary Technology and Internal Systems") and/or by email to your Authorized Representative — as coordinated with you at the start of the engagement.
- **Your response time on deliverables.** When you receive a deliverable for approval, the default is a defined and final written response from you within three (3) Business Days of delivery, unless a different time was set in the Proposal (Section 4(a) of the Agreement) — so that we can both keep a reasonable working pace.
- **Entering a new channel requires your approval.** Even within the approved budget, entering a new marketing or commercial channel that was not included in the approved plan requires your express prior written approval — just like a drop below the profitability/Break-Even targets beyond the Tolerance level you set (Appendix B, Section 2(c)).
- **What Happens if the Stop-Loss Threshold Is Crossed.** Crossing the threshold actually stops or scales back the activity, to halt further loss — not merely to flag it. You will be informed and will receive an explanation of the cause and of the options going forward; resuming activity requires your approval. The exact timelines, the content of the notice and your right to a review meeting — Appendix B, Section 3(b) of the Agreement.
- **You can stop at any time.** A stop you initiate, within the framework set by the Agreement, is not considered a breach or a budget reduction, and does not reduce the consideration already accrued; the exact scope (including the cap on days per month) — Appendix B, Section 3(c)–(d) of the Agreement.
